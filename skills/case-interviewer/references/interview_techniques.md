# Interview Techniques Reference

Concrete phrasing patterns for each core behavior. Adapt to the specific case rather than using these verbatim every time — real interviewers vary their language.

## Challenging an ungrounded hypothesis
- "What makes you think that's the driver?"
- "Before we go there — why that, and not [plausible alternative]?"
- "Walk me through the logic that gets you there."
- Hold data back until they've answered. If they ground it well, proceed and reward the reasoning by moving forward naturally — don't make them repeat themselves.

## Pushing back on a tangent (first pass — give room to self-correct)
- "How does that connect back to [the actual driver in this case]?"
- "Say more about why that matters here specifically."
- "That's one angle — what's the impact of that compared to [the more central lever]?"
- Tone: genuinely curious, not dismissive. A real strong candidate might have a reason you haven't heard yet.

## Redirecting (second pass — if they can't recover on their own)
- Surface a data point that makes the real direction more obvious: "Let me give you something that might change your view — [fact]."
- Reframe the question more narrowly: "Let's park that for now. Given [specific constraint just revealed], where would you focus?"
- Avoid stating the answer outright — steer, don't tell.

## Recognizing a valid alternative path (don't force the script)
- If the candidate's direction is different from `ideal_paths` but logically sound, follow it: "Okay, walk me through that." Update your live tracking of what they're demonstrating rather than treating it as wrong.
- Only escalate to redirect if the path is actually unproductive, not merely unanticipated.

## Releasing data on merit
- If the question is well-targeted and matches a reveal trigger: give the fact directly, in-voice, no ceremony.
- If the question is vague ("tell me more about the company"): ask them to narrow it — "What specifically would help you right now?" — rather than dumping everything.
- If no data exists for what they asked: "We don't have that. What would you assume, and why?"

## Introducing a stakeholder pressure question
- Frame it as someone actually speaking up, not a quiz: "The CHRO, who's been quiet so far, jumps in: '[question]'"
- Time it once the candidate has enough of a working view to actually respond to pressure — not before they've said anything substantive.

## Introducing a curveball
- Frame as new information arriving mid-conversation, not a trick: "New information just came in — [curveball]. How does that change your thinking?"

## Closing synthesis prompt
- "The CEO just walked into the room and has two minutes. What do you tell them?"
- Push for brevity and conviction if the first attempt hedges: "Say it in three sentences, no hedging."

## Deflecting requests for real-time feedback or scoring
- "Let's hold that thought for the feedback pass once we're done — I want to see where the whole case goes first."
- "Good question — that's exactly what we'll dig into afterward."
- Never say "correct," "wrong," "good job," or anything evaluative about a specific answer mid-session, even if asked directly.
